\section{Notation}

Assuming Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$, with $z$ vertical and $x,y$ horizontal (maybe not very strict mathematically).
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gradient: $\nabla f:= (\p_x f, \p_y f, \p_z f)$ (vector)
    \item Divergence: $ \nabla \, . \mathbf{u} := \p_x u_x + \p_y u_y + \p_z u_z$ (scalar), where $\mathbf{u} = (u_x, u_y, u_z)$
    \item Material derivative: $\frac{D f}{D t} := \p_t f + \mathbf{u} \, . \, \nabla f = \p_t f + u_x \p_x f + u_y \p_y f  + u_z \p_z f$ (scalar), where $\mathbf{u}$ is the fluid velocity wrt an Eulerian observer. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Terminology}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Strain rate tensor: $\dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \p_i u_j + \p_j u_i \right)$
    \item Deviatoric stress tensor: $\tau_{ij} = \sigma_{ij} - \frac{1}{3} \sigma_{kk} \delta_{ij}$ (Einstein summation notation used): the stress tensor minus the isotropic part (pressure) (how much the stress state deviated from being isotropic). 
    \item Rheology: relation between force and deformation of material. For mathematical modelling that is a stress and velocity relation. The velocity itself is not relevant since it can change by changing frame. The relative velocity of neighbouring points is relevant, and is captured by the strain rate tensor.
    \item Prescribed boundary condition: a boundary condition where the value of the variable is set (e.g. via Dirichlet BC). Typically, for grounded ice, the bed is a prescribed boundary.
    \item Free boundary condition: a boundary condition where the value of the variable is not set, but determined as part of the solution. Typically, for grounded ice, the ice surface is a free boundary. For floating ice, both the lower and upper surfaces, as well as the calving front, are free boundaries.
\end{itemize}
\section{Full Stokes Equations}

See sec. 1.4.4. of \cite{karthaus_notes}.
\\
The Navier-Stokes equations for an incompressible viscous Newtonian fluid (constant viscosity $\eta$). They come from considerations of conservation of mass and momentum within a fixed volume of fluid material (see sec. 1.3 and 1.4.2 of \cite{karthaus_notes}, respectively), and are given by:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \nabla \, . \mathbf{u} &= 0 \,, 
    \hspace{4cm} \text{(mass equation)}
    \label{NS:mass_eq}
    \\
    \rho \frac{D \mathbf{u}}{D t} &= 
    \rho \mathbf{g} - \nabla p + \eta \nabla^2 \mathbf{u}
    \,,
    \qquad \text{(momentum equation)}
    \label{NS:mom_eq}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\rho$ is the density, $\mathbf{u}$ the fluid velocity, $p : - \sigma_{ii}/3$ the pressure, $\eta$ the viscosity, and $\mathbf{g}$ the gravitational acceleration vector. In the momentum equation it was used that for a \textbf{Newtonian fluid} the stress tensor is given by 
\begin{align}    
    \sigma_{ij} = - p \delta_{ij} + \eta \left( \p_i u_j + \p_j u_i \right) \,.
\end{align}
This was a simplifying assumption, in general there is a version of NS that does not use this, but more generically can have the full stress tensor. In the form above, the NS system is a set of 4 equations for 4 unkowns ($p, \mathbf{u}$). The non-linear term $(\mathbf{u} . \nabla) \mathbf{u}$ makes the equations hard to solve.
\\[10pt]
See sec. 8.2 of \cite{karthaus_notes}.

Ice has a low Reynolds number and the inertial, as well as the acceleration terms ($\p_t u$) are ignored (TODO: find the explicit form of the Navier-Stokes eqs and the explicit step that leads to Stokes for low Re). This results in the following equatiations for incompressible, slow ice flow
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \nabla . \mathbf{u} & = 0
    \,, 
    \label{Stokes:mass_eq}
    \\
    0 & = \rho \mathbf{g} - \nabla p + \nabla . \mathbf{\tau}
    \,,
    \label{Stokes:mom_eq}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
which are called Stokes equations (or Stokes flow). Notice the difference between \eqref{NS:mom_eq} and \eqref{Stokes:mom_eq}: ice is not a Newtonian fluid (viscosity is not constant with temperature), and therefore the full stress is used (split in pressure $p$ and the deviatoric stress $\tau$).
In ice modelling the Stokes equations are supplemented by a Rheology relation, such that they form a system of equations for 4 unknowns ($p, \mathbf{u}$). A common rheology relation is Glen's law:
\begin{align}
    \dot{\epsilon}_{ij} = A \tau^{n-1} \tau_{ij} \,,
    \qquad
    \tau = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} \tau_{ij} \tau_{ij}} \,,
\end{align}
where $\tau$ is the second invariant of the deviatoric stress tensor, $A(T)$ is a temperature dependent rate factor, and $n$ is a constant (usually taken as 3 for ice). Glen's law is non-linear. For a Newtonian fluid (a good approximation for water or air) a linear relation holds
\begin{align}
    \tau_{ij} = 2 \eta \, \dot{\epsilon}_{ij} \,,
\end{align}
where $\eta$ is the constant viscosity. In addition to the rheology relation, one also needs to know the behaviour of the ice temperature $T$, as it appears in the rate factor $A(T)$. The behaviour of $T$ van be found via temperature and energy considerations that result in the equations
\begin{align}

    & = 
    \nabla . \left( k \nabla T \right) + \tau_{ij} \dot{\epsilon}_{ij} \,,
\end{align}

\section{Shallow Ice Approximation (SIA)}

\subsection{Theory}

SIA is a zeroth order approximation (typically). The expansion parameter is the aspect ratio between depth and horizontal length scale, which is small for ice sheets. Physically, the assumption is that variation in the horizontal coordinate (x,y) is much smaller in the vertical (z). To zeroth order the components of the strain rate tensor are
\begin{align}
     \dot{\epsilon} = 
     \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & 0 & \p_z u_x / 2 \\
        0 & 0 & \p_z u_y / 2 \\
        \p_z u_x / 2 & \p_z u_y / 2 & 0
     \end{pmatrix}
     \,,
\end{align}
or in terms of the deviatoric stress, the only non-zero components are $\tau_{xz}, \tau_{yz}$.
The momentum equations reduce to
\begin{align}
    \p_x p = \p_z \tau_{xz} \,,
    \quad
    \p_y p  = \p_z \tau_{yz} \,,
    \quad
    \p_z p & = - \rho g \,,
\end{align}
And the surface boundary conditions are
\begin{align}
    p = \tau_{xz} = \tau_{yz} = 0.
\end{align}
The last of the momentum equations is just an ODE, which can be integrated on its own to yield the pressure profile
\begin{align}
    p = \rho g (s - z) \,,
\end{align}
where $s(x,y)$ is the surface (free boundary). Then, this solution can be plugged in the first two momentum equations, which after intergration in z (from z to surface) since the explicit z-profile is known, yield
\begin{align}
    \tau_{xz}  = - \rho g (s - z) \p_x s \,,
    \quad
    \tau_{yz}  = - \rho g (s - z) \p_y s \,.
\end{align}
The ice thickness is defined as $h(x,y) := s(x,y) - b(x,y)$, where $b(x,y)$ is the bed (prescribed boundary), and the depth-integrated ice flux as
\begin{align}
    q_x := \int_b^s u_x \, dz
    \,,
    \quad
    q_y := \int_b^s u_y \, dz
    \,.
\end{align}
After z-integration (from b to s) of the mass equation we get
\begin{align}
    \int_b^s \left( \p_x u_x + \p_y u_y \right) dz + u_z |_b^s = 0 \,,
\end{align}
which combined with the boundary conditions
\begin{align}
    u_x \p_x b + u_y \p_y b = u_z \quad 
    & \text{at} \quad z=b
    \,,
    \\
    \p_t s + u_x \p_x s + u_y \p_y s = u_z + a \quad 
    & \text{at} \quad z=s
\end{align}
where $a(x,y)$ is the surface mass balance (positive for accumulation, negative for ablation), results in the SIA ice thickness equation
\begin{align}
    \p_t h + \p_x q_x + \p_y q_y = a \,.
\end{align}

Given a flow law (e.g. Glen's) and a sliding law, the fluxes can be expressed in terms of the ice thickness and surface elevation (and bed elevation for sliding). The result can be replaced in the ice thickness equation to get a closed equation for $h(x,y,t)$, which has a diffusion-like form.

We assume \textbf{no sliding}, a \textbf{flat bed}, a mass balance $a(x,y) = 0$, to arrive at
\begin{align}
    \p_t h

    D

    \,.

\end{align}

\subsection{Numerical implementation}

The time integration is performed with an explicit scheme with adaptive step (see sec. 8.3.1 of \cite{karthaus_notes}). The stability condition is 
\begin{align}
    \Delta t \leq \frac{\min(\Delta x, \Delta y)^2}{2 \max(D)}
    \,,
    \label{eq:2d_stability_condition}
\end{align}
and is based on a centred FD approximation in space and a forward in time. To understand it consider the 1d case
\begin{align*}
    \p_t T &= D \p_x^2 
    \\
    \rightarrow
    \frac{T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n}{\Delta t} 
    & = D \left( \frac{T_{j+1}^n - 2 T_{j^n} + T_{j-1}^n}{\Delta x^2} \right)
    \\
    \rightarrow
    T_j^{n+1} & = \mu T_{j+1}^n + \left(1 - 2 \mu \right) T_j^n + \mu T_{j-1}^n
    \,, \quad \text{with} \quad \mu:= D \Delta t / \Delta x^2
    \,.
\end{align*}
The latter provides stability if the rhs acts as an average, which is true when the term $(1-2\mu)$ is positive. This leads to the condition
\begin{align*}
    1-2\mu \geq 0 \rightarrow \Delta t \leq \frac{\Delta x^2}{2 D}
    \,,
\end{align*}
which generalized to \eqref{eq:2d_stability_condition} in 2d (where we take $\max{D}$ since it is not a constant as in the previous derivation). In practise we choose
\begin{align*}
    \Delta t = \frac{\min(\Delta x, \Delta y)^2}{4 \max(D)}
    \,,
\end{align*}
to safely saturate the bound.

For the explicit scheme to be conditionally stable (under condition \eqref{eq:2d_stability_condition}) the diffusion-like term $D(x,y)$ has to be evaluated on staggered grid points. This results in the following spatial discretization
\begin{align}
    \nabla . \left(D \nabla H \right) 
    &= 
    \frac{D_{i+1/2,j} (h_{i+1,j}-h_{i,j}) - D_{i-1/2,j}(h_{i,j}-h_{i-1,j})}{\Delta x^2}
    \nonumber
    \\
    & + 
    \frac{D_{i,j+1/2} (h_{i,j+1}-h_{i,j}) - D_{i,j-1/2}(h_{i,j}-h_{i,j-1})}{\Delta y^2}
    \,, 
    \label{eq:bueler_stencil}
\end{align}
which can be achieved if the inside derivative is forward (backward) and the outside backward (forward). To evaluate $D$ on staggered grid points, we use midpoint for the term without derivative, and a combination of midpoint and forward, backward, and centred FD for the term with derivative (see \cite{Karthaus_mfiles})

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.475\textwidth]{figs/grids.pdf}
    \caption{Staggered grid points. The dashed square indicates the cell (control volume $\Omega$) for the finite volume method used.}
    \label{fig:grid}
\end{figure}

To arrive at stencil \eqref{eq:bueler_stencil} we start from the strong form
\begin{align*}
    \p_t h = \nabla . \left( D \nabla h \right) 
\end{align*}
and integrate over a control volume $\Omega$ (fixed in time) to obtain
\begin{align*}
    \p_t \int_\Omega h \, d \Omega = \int_\Omega \, \nabla . \left( D \nabla h \right) d \Omega
    =
    \int_{\p \Omega} (D \nabla h) . \mathbf{n} \, dA
    \,,
\end{align*}
where we have used the divergence theorem and $\mathbf{n}$ is the unit normal vector that points outward of the control volume. Then we split the boundary of the control volume $\p \Omega$ in its components, to obtain
\begin{align}
    \p_t \int_\Omega h d\Omega = 
    \int_{\p \Omega_n} D \frac{\p h}{\p y} dA_{n}
    - \int_{\p \Omega_s} D \frac{\p h}{\p y} dA_{s}
    + \int_{\p \Omega_e} D \frac{\p h}{\p x} dA_{e}
    - \int_{\p \Omega_w} D \frac{\p h}{\p x} dA_{w}
    \,,
    \label{eq:sia_vol_integrated}
\end{align}
with $n,s,e,w,$ indicating north, south, etc wrt to the $i,j$ grid point, which we call $P$.

Let us take a closer look at the north part of $\p \Omega$ to understand the discretization. The length of that face is $\Delta x$, so the integral can be writen as
\begin{align*}
    \int_{-\Delta x/2}^{\Delta x/2} D \frac{\p h}{\p y} dx
    \,.
\end{align*}
To approximate this integral we are using midpoint approximation, which assumes that the integrand is constant across the face and it evaluates it at the face centre, which we call here N and has indices $i,j+1/2$. We end up with the following
\begin{align*}
    \int_{-\Delta x/2}^{\Delta x/2} D \frac{\p h}{\p y} dx
    \simeq
    D|_N \, \left[\frac{\p h}{\p y}\right]_{N} \, \Delta x
    \,.
\end{align*}
Based on this, we see that $D$ has to be evaluated on $N$ which is a staggered grid point. We also need to evaluate the derivative $\p h/\p y$ on $N$, which we do by using a centred (wrt $i,j+1/2$) stencil:
\begin{align*}
    \left[\frac{\p h}{\p y}\right]_{N} \simeq \frac{h_{i,j+1} + 0 \cdot h_{i,j+1/2} - h_{i,j}}{2 \cdot \Delta y/2}
    \,,
\end{align*}
which combined with the previous yields
\begin{align}
    \int_{\p \Omega_n} D \frac{\p h}{\p y} \, dA_n 
    \simeq D_{i,j+1/2} \frac{h_{i,j+1}-h_{i,j}}{\Delta y} \Delta x
    \,.
    \label{eq:int_north}
\end{align}
A similar process yields for following for the south (S), east (e), and west (W) points (wrt P):
\begin{align}
    \int_{\p \Omega_s} D \frac{\p h}{\p y} \, dA_s 
    & 
    \simeq D_{i,j-1/2} \frac{h_{i,j}-h_{i,j-1}}{\Delta y} \Delta x
    \,,
    \label{eq:int_south}
    \\
    \int_{\p \Omega_e} D \frac{\p h}{\p x} \, dA_e 
    & 
    \simeq D_{i+1/2,j} \frac{h_{i+1,j}-h_{i,j}}{\Delta x} \Delta y
    \,,
    \label{eq:int_east}
    \\
    \int_{\p \Omega_w} D \frac{\p h}{\p x} \, dA_w 
    & 
    \simeq D_{i-1/2,j} \frac{h_{i,j}-h_{i-1,j}}{\Delta x} \Delta y
    \,.
    \label{eq:int_west}
\end{align}
Replacing \eqref{eq:int_north}, \eqref{eq:int_south}, \eqref{eq:int_east}, and \eqref{eq:int_west} in \eqref{eq:sia_vol_integrated}, and deviding by the cell volume $\Delta x \Delta y$, we obtain
\begin{align}
    \p_t \bar{h}
    &=
    \frac{D_{i,j+1/2}(h_{i.j+1}-h_{i,j}) - D_{i,j-1/2}(h_{i,j}-h_{i,j-1})}{\Delta y^2}
    \nonumber
    \\
    &+
    \frac{D_{i+1/2,j}(h_{i+1,j}-h_{i,j}) - D_{i-1/2,j}(h_{i,j}-h_{i-1,j})}{\Delta x^2}
    \,,
\end{align}
where $\bar{h} : = \int_{\Omega} h d \Omega / (\Delta x \Delta y)$ is the mean value of $h$ within the control volume $\Omega$. The rhs is the stencil \eqref{eq:bueler_stencil} and in the derivation the 2nd order accurate centred FD operators have been used, for $\Delta x/2$ and $\Delta y/2$.

The quantity $D$ that needs to be evaluated on staggered points, is given by
\begin{align*}
    D = \Gamma h^5 |(\p_x h)^2 + (\p_y h)^2|
    \,, \quad
    \text{with}
    \quad
    \Gamma := \frac{2 A (\rho g)^3}{5}
    \,.
\end{align*}
On a staggered point with $i + 1/2,j$ the term $\p_x$ is approximated by a centred FD using the points $i,j$ and $i + 1,j$ (with spacing $\Delta x/2$), while the $\p_y$ term by a centred FD using the staggered points $i + 1/2, j + 1$ and $i + 1/2, j - 1$ (with spacing $2 \Delta y$):
\begin{align*}
    \p_x h|_{i+1/2,j}
    &\simeq
    \frac{h_{i+1,j}-h_{i,j}}{\Delta x} + O(\Delta x^2)
    \,,
    \\
    \p_y h|_{i+1/2,j}
    &\simeq
    \frac{h_{i+1/2,j+1}-h_{i+1/2,j-1}}{2 \Delta y} + O(\Delta y^2)
    \,,
\end{align*}
and similarly for the other staggered points. The approximations we use induce the following truncation errors
\begin{align*}
    f'(x) 
    &\simeq
    \frac{f_{i+1}-f_{i-1}}{2 \Delta x} + \frac{\Delta x ^2}{6} f'''(x) + O(\Delta x^3)
    \,,
    \\
    \bar{f} : = \frac{1}{\Delta x} \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} f(x) dx
    & \simeq
    \frac{f_i + f_{i+1}}{2} + \frac{\Delta x^2}{24}f''(x) + O(\Delta x^3)
    \,.
\end{align*}
The truncation error for stencil \eqref{eq:bueler_stencil} is second order, but it is multiplied with second and third derivatives of $h$ several times (due to the non-linearity of $D$) and so the converegence rate of a numerical test depends on these as well.

\section{Shallow Shelf Approximation (SSA)}

SSA applies well to floating ice shelves (no resistance from water underneath) and away from the grounding zone and calving front, as well as ice streams that are subject to small resistance from their base. The physical assumption is that normal stresses are dominant and shear stresses are neglected (in contrast to SIA).

The original derivation of the 2nd model can be found in \cite{MacAyeal_SSA}. The calculation includes series expansions both to 0th and 1st order (not sure what order the model is). Here we work with the simpler 1d model shown in sec. 8.7.1 of \cite{karthaus_notes}
\begin{align}
    \p_x \left( 2 \bar{v} H \p_x u \right) - \tau_b\, u = \rho g H \p_x s
    \,,
    \label{eq:SSA_1d}
\end{align}
with
\begin{align*}
    \bar{u} 
    &:= B | \p_x u |^{\frac{1}{n}-1} 
    \,,
    \quad \text{effective viscosity}\,,
    \\
    \tau_b 
    &:= C |u|^{m-1}
    \,,
    \quad \text{basal resistance}\,,
\end{align*}
and the negative of the rhs the driving stress due to gravity (movement of ice on its surface slope $\p_x s$ under its own weight, with $s(x)$ the surface). 

\subsection{Floating shelf: analytical solution}

For a floating shelf there is no basal resistance. The flotation criterion is $\rho H < - \rho_w b$, where $\rho$ is the (uniform) ice shelf density, $\rho_w$ the water density, $z=0$ the sea-surface level, $b(x)<0$ the bedrock, and $H(x)$ the total ice thickness. The surface of the shelf is $s(x)$ and due to hydrostatic equilibrium (balance of buoyancy and gravity) it is given by $s = (1-\rho/\rho_w)H$. Considering all this, the SSA model reads
\begin{align}
    \p_x \left( 2 \bar{v} H \p_x u \right) = \rho g \left(1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_w}\right) H \p_x H
    \,,
    \label{eq:SSA_1d_shelf}
\end{align}
which can be written as a total derivative in both hand-sides and integrated to given
\begin{align*}
    \p_x u = \left[ \frac{\rho g}{4 B} \left(1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_w}\right)\right]^n H^n
    \,,
\end{align*}
where the effective viscosity definition is used and solved for $\p_x u$. The integration constant has been set to zero (force balance at the calving front(?); see below eq 8.36 in \cite{karthaus_notes}). For a steady 1d ice shelf with constant thickness ($\p_t H = 0$) the mass continuity yields $M = \p_x (u H)$ which leads to
\begin{align*}
    u H = Q(x) = \int_{x_g}^{x}M dx + Q_I\,,
\end{align*}
where $x_g$ is the grounding line, and $Q_I$ the ice flux coming from the ice sheet upstream. The exact solution depends on the ice thickness $H_g$ and velocity $u_g$ at the grounding line; note $Q_I = H_g u_g$. In this case we choose a positive constant surface balance $M_0$, and obtain
\begin{align*}
    H(x) = \frac{1}{u(x)}\left[M_0 (x-x_g) + H_g u_g \right]
    \,,
\end{align*}
which leads to the following analytical solution for $u(x)$
\begin{align*}
    u(x)^{n+1} = \frac{1}{M_0} \left[ \frac{\rho g}{4 B} \left(1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_w}\right)\right]^n 
        \left\{
        \left[ M_0 (x-x_g) + H_g u_g \right]^{n+1} 
        - (H_g u_g)^{n+1}
        \right\}
        + u_g^{n+1}
        \,.
\end{align*}
In our setup we choose $x_g=0$ for simplicity, and $n=3$ (Glen's law).

\subsection{Floating shelf: numerical implementation}

We assume that the ice thickness $H$ is known. For the 1d problem described by eq. \eqref{eq:SSA_1d_shelf} we need to solve a boundary value problem for a non-linear ODE (it becomes an eliptic PDE in 2d). We use a Picard-type iterative algorithm, in which we assume a solution at step k-1 that provides the values for the coefficients, in order to solve for the unkown (speed $u(x)$ in this case) at step k. Then this solution is used to calculate the coefficients at step k, and repeat the process, namely
\begin{align}
    \p_x \left(W^{(k-1)} \p_x u^{(k)} \right) = \beta
    &\,,
    \label{eq:ssa_1d_shelf_numerics}
    \\
    \text{where} \quad
    W = 2 H B |\p_x u|^{\frac{1}{n}-1}
    &\,,
    \quad
    \beta = \rho g (1-\frac{\rho}{\rho_w})H \p_x H
    \nonumber
\end{align}
with boundary conditions for $u$ and/or $u_x := \p_x u$ at $x=x_g = 0$ and $x=x_c$:
\begin{align*}
    u(x_g) &= u_g 
    \,\ \\
    u_x(x_c) &= \gamma = \left[ \frac{\rho g}{4 B} \left(1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_w}\right) H(x_c)\right]^n
\end{align*}
where $\gamma$ is obtained from the calving front condition (which means what?)
\begin{align*}
    2 B H |u_x|^{\frac{1}{n}-1} u_x = \frac{1}{2} \rho \left(1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_w}\right) g H^2
    \,,
\end{align*}
assuming $u_x(x_c) \geq 0$. Eq. \eqref{eq:ssa_1d_shelf_numerics} is discretized using finite volume methods. A similar process with the SIA implementation (integration over a test volume and divergence theorem) yields
\begin{align}
    \frac{W_{i+1/2}(u_{i+1}-u_i) - W_{i-1/2}(u_i - u_{i-1})}{\Delta x^2} = \beta_i
    \label{eq:ssa_1d_shelf_numerics_discretized}
\end{align}
where for shortness we denote the rhs by $\beta$ (in case of non-zero basal resistance there is an additional term $-\alpha_i u_i$ on the lhs). The equation is then cast in the matrix form $\mathbf{A \, u = b}$ and its solution is $\mathbf{ u = A^{-1} \,b}$. The elements of $\mathbf{A,b}$ on each row $i$ are given by
\begin{align*}
    A_{i,i-1} = W_{i-1/2}
    \,,
    \quad
    A_{i,i} = - W_{i+1/2} - W_{i-1/2}
    \,,
    \quad
    A_{i,i+1} = W_{i+1/2}
    \,,
    \quad
    b_{i} = \Delta x^2 \beta_i 
\end{align*}
To apply the boundary conditions we need to change by hand the first and last rows of $\mathbf{A}$ as well as the first and last elements of $\mathbf{b}$. Let us denote as $i=1,N$ the points of the x grid. The boundary conditions are implemented with the following choices
\begin{align*}
    &\text{grounding line:} 
    \quad
    A_{1,1}=1 
    \,,
    A_{1,2 \dots N}=0
    \,, \quad
    b_1 = u_g
    \,,
    \\
    &\text{calving front:}
    \quad
    A_{N,N-1} = W_{N+1/2} + W_{N-1/2} = - A_{N,N}
    \,, \quad
    b_N = - 2 \Delta x \, W_{N+1/2} \, \gamma + \Delta x^2 \beta_N
    \,,
\end{align*}
where for the calving front we used an ghost point (non-populated, only for derivation) at point $i=N+1$ to evaluate the centred derivative of $u$ at $x=x_c$ as $(u_{N+1}-u_{N-1})/(2 \Delta x) = \gamma$, where the value of $u_{N+1}$ was calculated eq. \eqref{eq:ssa_1d_shelf_numerics_discretized} with $i=N$ and solving for $u_{N+1}$. Since $W_{N+1/2}$ is outside the computational domain, we assume $W_{N+1/2} = W_{N-1/2}$ (copy its value).

\section{Fracture}

For the fracture model we use the phase field model of \cite{Miehe2010}
\begin{align}
    \textrm{Div}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{u},d)] + \bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} 
    &= 0
    \,,
    \label{eq:miehe_solid_balance_eq}
    \\
    \frac{g_c}{l}
    \left( d - l^2 \Delta d \right) 
    & = 2(1-d) \mathcal{H}
    \,
    \label{eq:miehe_phase_field_eq}
\end{align}
where $\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}$ is the loading of the bulk of the material, $d$ the phase field, $l$ the chosen regularization length, $\mathcal{H}$ the history field, and $g_c$ is the Griffith-type critical energy release rate. For the phase field, $d=0$ means the solid is completely intact and $d=1$ completely damaged.
\\[10pt]
Below is a list of relevant definitions/notions:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\psi(\bb{\epsilon},d) = [g(d) + k] \psi_0^+(\bb{\epsilon}) + \psi_0^-(\bb{\epsilon})$ 
with $\psi$ the energy storage function that describes the energy stored in the bulk of the solid per unit volume (isotropic assumption for this form), $\psi_0$ is the isotropic reference energy function associated with the undamaged solid, and the positive/negative parts corresponde to tension and comporession related energies.

\item

$k \approx 0 $ is a small positive parameter used to circumvent the full degradation of the energy leaving the artificial elastic rest energy density $k \psi_0(\bb{\epsilon})$ at the fully broken state $d=1$. It is chosen as small as possible such that the algebraic conditioning number of the applied numerical discretization method remains well-posed for partly-broken systems.

\item The monotonically decreasing degredation function $g(d)$ describes the degredation of the positive (tensile) part of the stored energy with evolving damage and has the properties
$g(0)=1, \, g(1)=0, \, g'(1)=0$. The first and second are the limits of the unbroken and fully broken phase. The last ensures that the energetic fracture force converges to a finite value if the damage converges to a fully broken $d=1$ state. \tg{proof somewhere in the paper supposedly}. A degradation function that satisfies these conditions is $g(d) = (1-d)^2$.

\item $\langle x \rangle_{\pm} := (x \pm |x|)/2$

\item Consider the spectral decomposition of $\bb{\epsilon}$ (basically diagonalize it). Then you get the principal strain $\{\epsilon^i\}_{i=1,\cdots,\delta}$ (eigenvalues) and principal strain directions $\{\mathbf{n}^i\}_{i=1,\cdots,\delta}$ (eigenvectors). In this decomposition (diagonal form) all the strains are normal (no shear), along the principal directions (basically a coordinate transformation). $\delta$ here denotes the total number of spatial dimensions considerred.

\item $\bb{\epsilon}_{\pm} := \sum_{i=1}^\delta \langle \epsilon^i \rangle_\pm \, \mathbf{n}^i \otimes \mathbf{n}^i$

\item $\psi_0(\bb{\epsilon}) := \lambda \, \text{tr}^2[\bb{\epsilon}]/2 + \mu \, \text{tr}[\bb{\epsilon}^2]$ is the isotropic reference energy function associated with the undamaged elastic solid, with $\lambda, \mu$ positive elastic constants.

\item It can also be decomposed to positive (tensile) and negative (comporessive) parts via 
\begin{align*}
    \psi_0(\bb{\epsilon}) = \psi_0(\bb{\epsilon})^+ + \psi_0(\bb{\epsilon})^-
    \,,
\end{align*}
with 
\begin{align*}

    \lambda \, \langle \text{tr}^2[\bb{\epsilon}]\rangle_+/2

    \lambda \, \langle \text{tr}^2[\bb{\epsilon}]\rangle_-/2

\end{align*}

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coupled with 1d elastic bar}

Setup: uniform, static, elastic rod of length $L$, with Dirichlet boundary conditions on the left and right ends (prescribed displacement). We want to couple this to the phase field. The domain is $x \in [0,L]$. The system of equations solved is
\begin{align}
    \p_x \left[ g(d) \sigma(u)_+ + \sigma(u)_- \right]
    &= 0
    \,,
    \label{eq:static_elastic_rod_1d}
    \\
    \frac{g_c}{l} \left(d(x) - l^2 \frac{d^2}{dx^2} d(x) \right)
    & = 2 \left( 1 - d(x) \right) \mathcal{H}(x)
    \,,
    \label{eq:1d_phase_field}
    \\
    \text{with the history field: }
    \mathcal{H}(x)
    & = \frac{1}{2} E \left( \frac{d u(x)}{dx} \right)^2
    \,, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $u_x := du(x)/dx$, $\sigma_+ = E \max(u_x, 0)$, $\sigma_- = E \min(u_x, 0)$ the split of stress into tensile and compressive in our 1D setup, $g(d) : = (1-d)^2 + k$ is the degradation function, $E$ Young's modulus, $g_c$ the critical energy for fracture, and $l$ the regularization length. The unknowns are the displacement field $u(x)$ and the phase field $d(x)$. The Dirichlet boundary conditions for the discplacement are $u(0)=\bar{u}(0)$ and $u(0)=\bar{u}(L)$, where $\bar{u}$ is the prescribed displacement. For the phase field we applied the following Neumann boundary considerations: $d_x(0) = d_x(L) = 0$ (basically no damage comes from outside?).

Take a cell of length $\Delta x$ from $x_{i-1/2}$ to $x_{i+1/2}$, then
\begin{align*}
    \overline{\left[ g(d) E \max(u_x,0) + E \min(u_x,0)\right]} \Delta x
    &:= 
    \int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}} \frac{d}{dx} \left[ g(d) E \max(u_x,0) + E \min(u_x,0) \right] dx

\end{align*}
where $\overline{[.]}$ denoting an average value within the cell, and
\begin{align*}

    \,, \quad
    g_{i+1/2} = \frac{g(d_{i+1}) + g(d_i)}{2}
    \,,
    \\
    N_{i-1/2} = g_{i-1/2} E \max(\frac{u_{i} - u_{i-1}}{\Delta x},0) + E \min(\frac{u_{i} - u_{i-1}}{\Delta x},0)
    \,, \quad
    g_{i-1/2} = \frac{g(d_{i}) + g(d_{i-1})}{2}
    \,.
\end{align*}

A_{i,i} = - E g_{i+1/2} - E g_{i-1/2}
\,,

A_{i,i+1} = E g_{i+1/2}

\begin{enumerate}

    \item Solve iteratively for each pseudotime step.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Viscoelastic model}

We follow \cite{stocek2023} and that details a model for viscoelastic ice that is subject to water pressure in cracks (they have water in when they reach water level). Below is the full system of equations for the bulk of the domain of interest $\Omega$ in a dimensionless form:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    - \nabla \cdot \bb{\sigma}
    & = C_1 \left[ g(d) \bb{f} -p_w \nabla g(d) \right]
    \,,
    \quad \text{(momentum eq)}
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:momentum}
    \\
    g(d) \, 2 \eta \, \dev(\nabla_s \bb{\dot{w}}) - 2 C_2 \, \dev(\bb{\sigma})
    & = 0
    \,,
    \hspace{4cm} \text{(viscosity rule)}
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:viscosity_rule}
    \\
    \nabla \cdot \left(\bb{u}-\bb{w}\right) + \frac{3 \left(1-2\nu\right)}{2 \left(1 + \nu\right)} p
    & = 0
    \,,
    \hspace{4.35cm} \text{(mass eq)}
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:mass}
    \\
    \nabla \cdot \bb{\dot{w}}
    & = 0
    \,,
    \hspace{3.6cm}
    \text{(incompressibility)}
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:incompressibility}
    \\
    d - l^2 \Delta d 
    & = C_3 \, l \, 2 \left(1 - d \right) \mathcal{H}
    \,,
    \hspace{2cm}
    \text{(phase field)}
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:phase_field}
\end{align}
\label{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs}
\end{subequations}
where $\dev$ is the deviatoric part of a tensor with $\dev(A)_{ij} = A_{ij} - \frac{1}{3} \delta_{ij} A_{kk}$ in index notation (and 3 dimensions), $\nabla_s$ the symmetrized gradient with $(\nabla_s v)_{ij} = (\p_i v_j + \p_j v_i)/2$ in index notation, $\eta=\eta(\dev(\nabla_s \bb{\dot{w}}))$, and $\bb{\sigma} = g(d) \bb{\sigma}_e^+ + \bb{\sigma}_e^-$ with $\bb{\sigma}_e^{\pm} := \langle -p \rangle_{\pm} \mathbb{1} + 2 \dev \, \left( \nabla_s(\bb{u-w})\right)_{\pm}$. Also, here $p$ is the volumetric part of $\sigma$ i.e. $p = - \text{tr}(\bb{\sigma}_v)/3$ (in 3d) and a Maxwell model is used for ice, where the total strain is decomposed additively into elastic and viscous strains, whereas the total Cauchy stress is equal in both elastic and viscous stresses
\begin{align*}
    \bb{\epsilon} = \bb{\epsilon}_e + \bb{\epsilon}_\varv
    \,,
    \\
    \bb{\sigma} = \bb{\sigma}_e = \bb{\sigma}_\varv
    \,.
\end{align*}

and if we want $\bb{\sigma}_e = \bb{\sigma}_\varv$ to hold, we get $\dot{w}_x = \mu (u_x - w_x)/\eta$. Also, as a sanity check, $p = - \tr(\bb{\sigma}_\varv) = -(\lambda + 2 \mu/3)(u_x - w_x)$.

For the first equation \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:momentum} we need the form of the split elastic stress tensor $\bb{\sigma} = g(d) \bb{\sigma}_e^+ + \bb{\sigma}_e^-$ into tension ($\bb{\sigma}_e^+$) and compression ($\bb{\sigma}_e^-$), with $\bb{\sigma} = g(d) \bb{\sigma}_e^+ + \bb{\sigma}_e^-$ with 
\begin{align*}
\bb{\sigma}_e^{\pm} := \langle -p \rangle_{\pm} \mathbb{1} + 2 \dev \, \left( \nabla_s(\bb{u-w})\right)_{\pm}
\,.
\end{align*}
First we have
\begin{align*}

    & = \langle \left(\lambda + \frac{2 \mu}{3} \right) \left(u_x  -w_x \right)\rangle_{\pm}
    = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(\lambda + \frac{2 \mu}{3} \right) \left(u_x -w_x \right) \pm \left| \left(\lambda + \frac{2 \mu}{3} \right) \left(u_x - w_x \right) \right| \right]
    \,.
\end{align*}
Then, using the definition $\bb{\epsilon} := \sum_i \langle e_i \rangle_{\pm} \bb{m}_i \otimes \bb{m}_i$ and $a_x := u_x - w_x$ for shorthand
\begin{align*}

    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
    \frac{4}{3} a_x & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & -\frac{2}{3} a_x & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & -\frac{2}{3} a_x

    \,,
\end{align*}
we have

The viscosity rule \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:viscosity_rule} $g(d) \, 2 \eta \, \dev(\nabla_s \bb{\dot{w}}) - 2 C_2 \, \dev(\bb{\sigma}) = 0$ is a matrix equation. We have that 
\begin{align*}
    \dev (\nabla_s \dot{\bb{w}}) = 
    \text{diag}
    \left(
        \frac{2}{3}\dot{w}_x \,, 
        -\frac{1}{3}\dot{w}_x \,,
        -\frac{1}{3}\dot{w}_x
    \right) 
\end{align*}
where the second and third non-zero elements are a multiple $-1/2$ of the first, as in $\dev(\bb{\sigma})$, which results in only one equation, that is
\begin{align*}
    2 g \, \eta \, \dot{w}_x 
    - 
    2 C_2 \, 
    \left[ \frac{4}{3} (g+1) (u_x-w_x)
    + \frac{4}{9} (g-1) |u_x-w_x|
    \right] 
    =0 
    \,,
\end{align*}
where $\eta = \eta(\dev(\nabla_s \dot{\bb{w}}))$.

For the mass equation \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:mass} $\nabla \cdot \left(\bb{u}-\bb{w}\right) + \frac{3 \left(1-2\nu\right)}{2 \left(1 + \nu\right)} p = 0$ we get
\begin{align*}
    (u_x - w_x) 
    \left[
        1 - \frac{3 \left(1-2\nu\right)}{2 \left(1 + \nu\right)}
        (\lambda + \frac{2\mu}{3})
    \right]
    = 0\,,
    \\
    \text{with}
    \qquad
    \lambda = \frac{E \nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}
    \,, \quad
    \mu = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}
    \,,
\end{align*}
which leads to the constraint between the chosen parameters $E = 2(1+\nu)^2/(1-\nu)$, if we demand that is satisfy for every $u_x-w_x$.

The incompressibility equation \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:incompressibility} $\nabla \cdot \dot{\bb{w}}$ in this setup reads $\dot{w}_x = \p_t \p_x w =0$, which is very restrictive in this setup, meaning that $w$ is a constant in time and space. We relax this assumption and do not satisfy it in this 1d setup. Physically, since the domain is allowed to move only in the x direction, its volume will have to change necessarily to have a non-trivial configuration.

Finally, the phase field equations \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_full_eqs:phase_field} $d-l^2 \Delta d = C_3 \, l \, 2 \left(1 - d \right) \mathcal{H}$ reads
\begin{align*}
    d - l^2 \p_x^2 d = C_3 \, l \, 2 (1-d) \mathcal{H}
    \,,
    \\
    \text{with } 
    \mathcal{H}(x,t) 
    :=
    \max_s \langle \psi^ + \left( \bb{e}_e (x,s) \right) - \psi_{crit} \rangle_+
    \\
    \text{and }
    \psi^+ = \frac{1}{2} \left(\lambda + \frac{2\mu}{3}\right)
    \langle \tr (\bb{\epsilon}_e) \rangle_+^2
    + \mu \, \tr \left( (\dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e)_+)^2 \right) 
\end{align*}
which reads
\begin{align*}
    \text{if } (u_x-w_x) >0: 
    &
    \quad
    \psi^+ = (u_x-w_x)^2 \left( \frac{\lambda}{2} + \frac{19 \mu}{9} \right)
    \\
    \text{if } (u_x-w_x) \leq 0: 
    &
    \quad
    \psi^+ = (u_x-w_x)^2 \frac{8 \mu}{9}
    \,.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Numerical implementation with implicit time step}

The recipe is the following: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Give initial data for $d_0,w_0,u_0$.
    \item Consider two consecutive timesteps $t_k$ and $t_{k-1}$ and the phase field known and fixed. Write eqs \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:momentum} and \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:viscosity} in the form $\bb{A} \cdot \bb{V} = \bb{b}$, where $\bb{V} = (\bb{u}^k,\bb{w}^k)^T$, $\bb{A}$ includes the $\p_t, \p_x$ operators for finite volume and implicit Euler, $\bb{b}$ includes the BC conditions for $u^k$(and $w^k$ BC?), and $d^{k-1}$ is used in $g(d)$. Solve with a fixed point iteration until convergence. The fixed point iterations are labelled with $j \in[1,J]$. A scalar variable that includes all the discrete labels looks like this: $f_i^{k,j}$.
    \item Use the results of above to solve the phase field eq and use that value of phase field in the next time step.
\end{enumerate}
We use finite volume discretization on a uniform spatial grid.

The form of eq. \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:momentum} is
\begin{align}
    \p_x \left[ \bar{K} \left(u_x - w_x\right) \right] = 0
    \,,
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_1d:momentum:form}
    \text{ with }
    \bar{K} 
    &
    = 2 g(d) \left(\frac{\lambda}{2} + \frac{\mu}{3} + \frac{4}{3} \right) 
    \text{ if } u_x-w_x >0
    \,,
    \\
    \text{ or }
    \bar{K} 
    &
    = 2 \left(\frac{\lambda}{2} + \frac{\mu}{3} + \frac{4}{3} \right)
    \hspace{0.7cm}
    \text{ if }
    u_x-w_x \leq 0
    \,,
    \nonumber
\end{align}
with $g=g(d)$. The dynamic viscosity comes from Glen's flow law
\begin{align}
    \eta = \eta(\dot{\bb{\epsilon}}_\varv) 
    = 
    \frac{1}{2} A^{-\frac{1}{n}} 
    \left(\frac{1}{2} \dot{\bb{\epsilon}}_\varv : \dot{\bb{\epsilon}}_\varv \right)^{\frac{1-n}{2n}}
    =
    \frac{1}{2} A^{-\frac{1}{n}} 
    \left[\frac{1}{2} (\dot{w}_x)^2 \right]^{\frac{1-n}{2n}}
    \,,
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_1d:Glen}
\end{align}
with $n=3$ and $A$ a constant that depends on temperature. The form of eq. \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:viscosity} is 
\begin{align*}
    \eta(\dot{w}_x) \dot{w}_x = \bar{F} (u_x-w_x)
\end{align*}
which after we replace the viscosity and solve for $\dot{w}_x$ (using $\dot{w}_x := \text{sgn}(\dot{w}_x) |\dot{w}_x|$)
\begin{align}
    \text{sgn}(\dot{w}_x)^n |\dot{w}_x| 
    = F \left(u_x - w_x \right)^n
    \,,
    \label{eq:viscoelastic_1d:viscosity:form}
    \text{ with }
    F = F(d)
    &
    = 2^{\frac{1+n}{2}} A 
    \left[C_2(\frac{16}{9} + \frac{8}{9 g(d)})\right]^n
    \text{ if } u_x - w_x >0
    \,,
    \\
    \text{ or }
    F =F(d)
    &
    = 2^{\frac{1+n}{2}} A 
    \left[C_2(\frac{8}{9} + \frac{16}{9 g(d)})\right]^n
    \text{ if } u_x - w_x \leq 0
    \,.
    \nonumber
\end{align}
We typically use $n=3$ and so the lhs is just $\dot{w}_x$. Here, $A$ is fixed during the simulation, and $g(d)$ are calculated in a timestep $t_k$ from the value of the phase field $d$ of the previous timestep $t_{k-1}$. Also, the degredation function used in the numerical implementation is $g(d) = (1-d)^2 + \kappa$ with $\kappa$ small (e.g. $\kappa = 10^{-6}$), to avoid division by zero in the event of a complete damage.

We want to write eqs. \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:momentum:form} and \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:viscosity:form} in the form $\bb{A} \cdot \bb{U} = \bb{b}$ with $\bb{U}=(\bb{u}^k,\bb{w}^k)^T$ the unkowns at timestep $t_k$ and $\bb{b}$ includes BC for $\bb{U}$. For \eqref{eq:viscoelastic_1d:momentum:form}, integrating over a cell labelled by $i$, with faces at $i \pm 1/2$, we have
\begin{align*}
    \int_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{i+\frac{1}{2}}
    \p_x \left[ \bar{K} (u_x-w_x)\right]
    &
    =
    \left[ \bar{K} (u_x-w_x)\right]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
    -
    \left[ \bar{K} (u_x-w_x)\right]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
    \\
    &
    =
    \bar{K}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} 
    \left( 
        \frac{u_{i+1}-u_i}{\Delta x} - \frac{w_{i+1}-w_i}{\Delta x}
    \right)
    -
    \bar{K}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} 
    \left( 
        \frac{u_i-u_{i-1}}{\Delta x} - \frac{w_i-w_{i-1}}{\Delta x}
    \right)
    \\
    &
    =
    \frac{1}{\Delta x}
    \left[
        \bar{K}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} u_{i+1} 
        - \left(\bar{K}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} +\bar{K}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\right) u_i 
        + \bar{K}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} u_{i-1}
    \right]
    \\
    & \quad
    -\frac{1}{\Delta x}
    \left[
        \bar{K}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} w_{i+1} 
        - \left(\bar{K}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} +\bar{K}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\right) w_i 
        + \bar{K}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} w_{i-1}
    \right]
    \,,
\end{align*}
which, to be consistent with a second derivative approximation, is devided with another $\Delta x$ that comes from the integration of the rhs (its value is approximated as constant in the cell and represented by its value at $i$; the volume comes from the itnegral, here just $\Delta x$ because we are in 1d). Here, the rhs is zero, so $\Delta x$ can be removed altogether.

    = \int_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{i+\frac{1}{2}} \p_x \left[ F (u-w) 
    G\right]
    \\
    & \quad
    - \int_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{i+\frac{1}{2}} \left( \p_x F \right) (u-w)
    G
    \\
    & \quad
    - \int_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{i+\frac{1}{2}} F  (u-w)
    \left( \p_x G \right)
    \\
    \rightarrow
    \p_t \left( w_{i+\frac{1}{2}} - w_{i-\frac{1}{2}} \right)
    & = 
    \left[F \, G (u-w)\right]_{i+\frac{1}{2}}
    - \left[F \, G(u-w)\right]_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
    - \left[F_x \, G (u-w)\right]_i \Delta x
    - \left[F \, G_x (u-w)\right]_i \Delta x
    \\
    \rightarrow
    \p_t \left(\frac{w_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{w_{i-1}}{2}\right)
    &
    =
    F_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G_{i+\frac{1}{2}} 
    \left(\frac{u_i+u_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{w_i+w_{i+1}}{2}\right)
    - F_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
    \left(\frac{u_i+u_{i-1}}{2} - \frac{w_i+w_{i-1}}{2}\right)
    \\
    & \quad 
    - F_{x,i} G_i (u_i-w_i) \Delta x
    - F_i G_{x,i} (u_i-w_i) \Delta x
    \\
    \rightarrow
    \p_t \left(\frac{w_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{w_{i-1}}{2}\right)
    &
    = 
    u_i 
    \left[ \frac{F_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
    - \frac{F_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
    - \left(F_{x,i} G_i + F_i G_{x,i}\right)\Delta x 
    \right]
    \\
    &
    \quad
    + u_{i+1} \frac{F_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    - u_{i-1} \frac{F_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    \\
    & \quad
    - w_i 
    \left[ \frac{F_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
    - \frac{F_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
    - \left(F_{x,i} G_i + F_i G_{x,i}\right)\Delta x 
    \right]
    \\
    &
    \quad
    - w_{i+1} \frac{F_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    + w_{i-1} \frac{F_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    \,,
\end{align*}
where we have split $(u_x-w_x)^n$ into $\p_x(u-w)G$ with $G:=(u_x-w_x)^{n-1}$ which is evaluated within a fixed point iteration $j$ using the solution of the previous iteration $j-1$, and $F=F(d)$ uses $d^{k-1}$ from the previous timestep $t_{k-1}$ for the fixed point iterations on timestep $t_k$. If $n=1$, then we have the linear rheology case and $G=1, G_x=0$, for which $J=1$ for the fixed point solver (no coefficients that depend on $u,w$ in front of $u,w$ unknowns). Formally we have
\begin{align*}
    G_x = (n-1)(u_x-w_x)^{n-2} (u_{xx}-w_{xx})
    \,, 
\end{align*}
which has no formal divergence for $n=3$ and can be computed using FDs for 1st and 2nd derivatives (maybe it is more accurate this way; this is what the code does currently). We can also calculate $G$ and then $G_x$ numerically, using centred finite difference (FD) operator for first order derivatives (with forward and backward stencils for $i=1,N$, respectively).

Reminder: $f^{k,j}_i$ means scalar variable $f$ at spatial grid point $i \in[0,N]$, timestep $t_k$, and fixed point iteration $j \in [1,J]$. For the implicit Euler step we have that $w^k = w^{k-1} + \Delta t \, \text{rhs}^k$. We use the notation $F^k_i = F(d^k_i)$ since there is only one step to solve for $d$ and not iterations, $G^j_i=G(u^{k,j}_i,w^{k,j}_i)$ and $G_{x,i}$ is the discretized version of $G_x$ after action with the aforementioned FD on $G$. We get the following
\begin{align*}
    &
    \frac{w^{k,j}_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{w^{k,j}_{i-1}}{2}
    = 
    \frac{w^{k-1,J}_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{w^{k-1,J}_{i-1}}{2}
    \\
    &
    \hspace{1cm}
    + \Delta t \,
    \left\{
    u^{k,j}_i 
    \left[ 
        \frac{F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
        - \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
        - \left(F^{k-1}_{x,i} G^{j-1}_i + F^{k-1}_i G^{j-1}_{x,i}\right)\Delta x
    \right]
    \right.
    \\
    &\hspace{1cm}

        - u^{k,j}_{i-1} \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    \right\}
    \\

    - \Delta t \,
    \left\{
    w^{k,j}_i 
    \left[ 
        \frac{F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
        - \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
        - \left(F^{k-1}_{x,i} G^{j-1}_i + F^{k-1}_i G^{j-1}_{x,i}\right)\Delta x
    \right]
    \right.
    \\
    &\hspace{1cm}
    \left.
        + w^{k,j}_{i+1} \frac{F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
        - w^{k,j}_{i-1} \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    \right\}
    \\
    \rightarrow \quad
    &
    u^{k,j}_{i-1} \Delta t \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    - u^{k,j}_{i} \Delta t 
    \left[ 
        \frac{F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
        - \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
        - \left(F^{k-1}_{x,i} G^{j-1}_i + F^{k-1}_i G^{j-1}_{x,i}\right)\Delta x
    \right]
    \\
    &
    - u^{k,j}_{i+1} \Delta t \frac{F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    \\
    & 
    - w^{k,j}_{i-1}  \frac{1 + \Delta t \, F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    + w^{k,j}_{i} \Delta t 
    \left[ 
        \frac{F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2} 
        - \frac{F^{k-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
        - \left(F^{k-1}_{x,i} G^{j-1}_i + F^{k-1}_i G^{j-1}_{x,i}\right)\Delta x
    \right]
    \\
    &
    + w^{k,j}_{i+1} \frac{1 + \Delta t  \, F^{k-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}} G^{j-1}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}
    \\
    & \qquad 
    = \frac{w^{k-1,J}_{i+1}}{2} - \frac{w^{k-1,J}_{i-1}}{2}
    \,.
\end{align*}

Some comments/observations from numerical experiments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The implicit timestep implementation with $u,w$ in fixed point iteration and $d$ on its own, is mostly stable. When it goes close to total failure (of the material) $d$ can just fast and if the code does not crash, the $w$ solution is noisy (typically) on the left of the breaking point. Notice that the left BC is Dirichlet. Also, this depends on the numerical constant $k$ as well as the regularizationlength $l$, with better results in terms of smoothness with increasing values.
    \item I tried to relax the $w$ Dirichlet BC with some approx. matching of the evolution eq; it was worse. Maybe experiment with different BC on the left altogether?
    \item By plugging in solutions to disrcetized equations (with FDs) and calculating residuals, I checked the quality of the solutions. The residual for the viscous equation (that has a genuine time component) grows a lot with time. Experimenting with explicit timestep and RK4, this gets better. However that implementation does not solve $u,w$ coupled, but does a fixed point loop for $u,d$ assuming $w$ known. In this case the residual of the first equation is worse typically. TODO: check if it can be implemented with $u,w$ solved coupled, as well as relax RK4 after the first 2 steps and used AB3; more economic.
    \item Tried monolithic algorithm for implicit implementation $u,w,d$ in the same fixed point loop, not better and more expensive.
    \item Also tried to write the viscous eq in a conservative form (i.e. hit it with $\p_x$ and then FV discretize), worse results.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2d elastic plate}

The total discplacement is only elastic and denoted as
\begin{align*}
    \bb{a} = \left( a_1(t,x,y), a_2(t,x,y), 0\right)
\end{align*}
with $a_1$ the displacement in the x direction and $a_2$ in y. The resulting strain tensor and its trace are
\begin{align*}
    \bb{\epsilon}_e = 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \p_x a_1 & \frac{\p_x a_2 + \p_y a_1}{2} & 0 \\
        \frac{\p_x a_2 + \p_y a_1}{2} & \p_y a_2 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 
    \end{pmatrix}
    \,, \quad
    \tr(\bb{\epsilon})_e = \p_x a_1 + \p_y a_2
    \,.
\end{align*}
We use the notation $a_{1x}:=\p_x a_1$ etc. for shorthand. The deviatoric part of the elastic strain tensor is
\begin{align*}
    \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e) = \bb{\epsilon}_e - \frac{1}{3} \tr(\bb{\epsilon}_e) \mathbb{1}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{2a_{1x}-a_{2y}}{3} & \frac{a_{2x} + a_{1y}}{2} & 0 \\
        \frac{a_{2x} + a_{1y}}{2} & \frac{-a_{1x}+2a_{2y}}{3} & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & \frac{-a_{1x}+a_{2y}}{3}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \,.
\end{align*}
The elastic stress tensor is
\begin{align*}
    \bb{\sigma}_e = 
    \lambda \, \tr(\bb{\epsilon}_e) \mathbb{1}+ 2 \mu \, \bb{\epsilon}_e
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        (\lambda + 2 \mu) a_{1x} + \lambda a_{2y} & \mu a_{2x} + \mu a_{1y} & 0 \\
        \mu a_{2x} + \mu a_{1y} & \lambda a_{1x} + (\lambda + 2 \mu) a_{2y} & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & \lambda a_{1x}+\lambda a_{2y}
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
and the pressure is
\begin{align*}
    p := - \tr(\bb{\sigma}_e)/3 =  -(\lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu) (a_{1x} + a_{2y})
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
    \langle - p \rangle_{\pm}
    =
    \frac{-p \pm |-p|}{2} 
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[(\lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu)(a_{1x} + a_{2y}) \pm 
    \left|(\lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu)(a_{1x} + a_{2y})\right| \right]
    \,.
\end{align*}
The spectrally decomposed deviatoric elastic strain tensor is
\begin{align*}
    &
    \text{spec}\left[ \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e) \right]
    = 
    \text{diag} \left(q_1, q_2, q_3 \right)
    \,,
\end{align*}
with eigenvalues
\begin{align*}
    &
    q_1 = \frac{a_{1x}+a_{2y}}{6} -
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        (a_{1y}+a_{2x})^2 + (a_{1x}-a_{2y})^2
    \right]^{1/2}
    \\
    &
    q_{2} = \frac{a_{1x}+a_{2y}}{6} +
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        (a_{1y}+a_{2x})^2 + (a_{1x}-a_{2y})^2
    \right]^{1/2}
    \,,
    \\
    &
    q_3 = \frac{-a_{1x} + a_{2y}}{3}
    \,.
\end{align*}
$\text{spec}\left[ \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e) \right]$ is diagonal in the basis of the principal directions, i.e. directions where stresses are only tensile or compressive, and there is no shear stress. Its relation to $\dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e)$ in the $x,y$ basis is
\begin{align*}
   \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e) = \bb{S} \, \text{spec}\left[ \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e) \right] \, \bb{S}^T
   \,,
\end{align*}
where conveniently $\bb{S}^T$ can be used instead of $\bb{S}^{-1}$, because $\dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e)$ is symmetric.

Along the principal directions (and not in $x,y$) the stress tensor is written
\begin{align*}
    \hat{\bb{\sigma}} 
    &
    = g(d) \hat{\bb{\sigma}}_e^+ + \hat{\bb{\sigma}}_e^-
    = g(d) \left[ \langle -p \rangle_+ \mathbb{1} + 2 \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e)_+ \right]
    + 
    \left[ \langle -p \rangle_- \mathbb{1} + 2 \dev(\bb{\epsilon}_e)_- \right]
    \\
    &
    = \text{diag} \left(\hat{\sigma}_{11}, \hat{\sigma}_{22}, \hat{\sigma}_{33} \right)
\end{align*}
where $\hat{.}$ is used to denote the principal direction basis, and
\begin{align*}
    \hat{\sigma}_{ii} 
    &
    = -\frac{g+1}{2}p + \frac{g-1}{2}|-p| + (g+1)q_i + (g-1)|q_i|
    \\
    &
    = - m_p \, p + 2 m_{q_i} \, q_i 
    \,, 
    \text{ for }
    i=1,2,3
    \,,
\end{align*}
and $m_p, m_{q_i}$ are masks that track tensile/compressive split, with
\begin{align*}
    m_p 
    &= 1 \text{ if } p \leq 0 
    \,, \quad 
    m_p = g \text{ if } p > 0 
    \\
    m_{q_i} 
    &= 1 \text{ if } q_i \leq 0 
    \,, \quad 
    m_{q_i} = g \text{ if } q_i > 0 
    \,,
\end{align*}
and $g(d) = (1 - d)^2$ the degradation function informed by the phase field.

We drop the $z$ component for convenience in what follows. We want to use finite volume methods in our discretization, on the Cartesian $x,y$ basis. To do that, we need the stress tensor that includes the tensile/compressive split wich utilized the principal directions, transformed back into $x,y$.

The momentum equation (with vanishing rhs i.e. no water pressure, no gravity) is $\nabla \cdot \bb{\sigma} = 0$, which when integrated over a square cell $\Omega$ of volume $d\Omega = \Delta x \cdot \Delta y$, with faces labelled by east, west, north, and south, reads
\begin{align*}
    \int_\Omega \nabla \cdot \bb{\sigma} d\Omega 
    & = \int_{\p \Omega} \bb{\sigma} \cdot \bb{n} dA
\end{align*}
where the lhs yields 3 equations ($\nabla$ can be represented by a vector and $\bb{\sigma}$ a matrix), so the rhs the same. We denote by $\bb{\sigma}_i$ the rows of $\bb{\sigma}$ in the $x,y,$ basis to obtain
\begin{align*}
    \int_{\p \Omega} \bb{\sigma}_1 \cdot \bb{n} dA
    & = \int_{\p \Omega_e} \sigma_{11} dA_e
    - \int_{\p \Omega_w} \sigma_{11} dA_w
    + \int_{\p \Omega_n} \sigma_{12} dA_n
    - \int_{\p \Omega_s} \sigma_{12} dA_s
    \\
    \int_{\p \Omega} \bb{\sigma}_2 \cdot \bb{n} dA
    & = \int_{\p \Omega_e} \sigma_{12} dA_e
    - \int_{\p \Omega_w} \sigma_{12} dA_w
    + \int_{\p \Omega_n} \sigma_{22} dA_n
    - \int_{\p \Omega_s} \sigma_{22} dA_s
\end{align*}
where the outward pointing normal vector to $\p\Omega_e$ is $n_x = (1,0,0)$ to $\p\Omega_w$ is $-n_x$, to $\p\Omega_n$ is $n_y=(0,1,0)$, and to $\p\Omega_s$ is $-n_y$. Following the setup depicted in Fig. \ref{fig:grid} we get
\begin{align*}
    \int_{\p \Omega_e} \sigma_{lk} dA_e 
    &\simeq 
    \int_{-\Delta y/2}^{\Delta y/2} \sigma_{lk}|_e dy = \sigma_{lk| i+\frac{1}{2},j} \, \Delta y
    \,,
    \\
    \int_{\p \Omega_w} \sigma_{lk} dA_w 
    &\simeq 
    \int_{-\Delta y/2}^{\Delta y/2} \sigma_{lk}|_w dy = \sigma_{lk| i-\frac{1}{2},j} \, \Delta y
    \\
    \int_{\p \Omega_n} \sigma_{lk} dA_n 
    &\simeq 
    \int_{-\Delta x/2}^{\Delta x/2} \sigma_{lk}|_n dy = \sigma_{lk| i,j+\frac{1}{2}} \, \Delta x
    \,,
    \\
    \int_{\p \Omega_s} \sigma_{lk} dA_s 
    &\simeq 
    \int_{-\Delta x/2}^{\Delta x/2} \sigma_{lk}|_s dy = \sigma_{lk| i,j-\frac{1}{2}} \, \Delta x
    \,,
\end{align*}
where the indices $l,k$ label the elements of $\bb{\sigma}$. Then, the momentum equations read
\begin{align*}

    = 0

    +
    \left(\sigma_{22| i,j+\frac{1}{2}}-\sigma_{22| i,j-\frac{1}{2}} \right) \, \Delta x
    &
    = 0 \,.
\end{align*}
